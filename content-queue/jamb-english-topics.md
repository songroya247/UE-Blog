<h2>1. Understanding the JAMB Use of English Syllabus</h2>

<p>Preparing for the Unified Tertiary Matriculation Examination (UTME) requires a thorough understanding of all <strong>JAMB English topics</strong> outlined in the official Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board syllabus. Use of English remains the compulsory subject for every candidate writing UTME, regardless of whether you intend to study Medicine, Engineering, Law, or Accounting. Scrambling through textbooks without a systematic breakdown of high-yield areas often leads to wasted study hours and avoidable mistakes on exam day.</p>

<p>Authored by Femi Solomon, updated for the current UTME examination cycle.</p>

<blockquote class="def">
<b>JAMB Use of English Syllabus</b> is the official academic framework published by the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board to define the scope, skills, literary texts, and grammatical competencies required of candidates seeking admission into Nigerian tertiary institutions.
</blockquote>

<h3>1.1. Official JAMB Syllabus Breakdown</h3>
<p>The Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board structures the English examination to evaluate two foundational competencies: effective communication in written English and readiness for academic work at the tertiary level. Rather than testing abstract grammatical memorization, the Board measures how well you interpret written passages, apply structural rules, discern phonetic variations, and analyze literary expressions.</p>

<h3>1.3. Key Areas Tested in JAMB English</h3>
<p>Every question on the CBT screen aligns directly with specific sub-topics detailed in the syllabus. To maximize your score, you must understand how examiners craft distractor options in multiple-choice questions. Examiners routinely include choices that sound correct in informal Nigerian English but violate standard grammatical rules.</p>

<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Comprehension Skills:</strong> Identifying topic sentences, inferring mood, isolating writer attitude, and synthesizing arguments across narrative or expository passages.</li>
<li><strong>Structural Accuracy:</strong> Applying rules of subject-verb agreement, relative pronouns, subjunctive mood, and modal verbs.</li>
<li><strong>Lexical Choice:</strong> Distinguishing precise contextual synonyms, opposite meanings (antonyms), and idiomatic register.</li>

<h2>2. Core JAMB English Topics: A Deep Dive</h2>

<p>Achieving a score above 80 in JAMB Use of English requires a detailed mastery of every sub-topic. Below is an exhaustive breakdown of the five core topic clusters tested in the examination.</p>

<h3>2.1. Reading Comprehension</h3>
<p>Reading comprehension assesses your capacity to absorb written prose, extract essential facts, and draw logical deductions under time pressure. The examination presents three main text formats in Section A:</p>

<h4>2.1.1. Types of Passages</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Expository Passages:</strong> Technical or informational articles explaining scientific concepts, environmental phenomena, economics, or social developments. They demand objective tracking of arguments.</li>
<li><strong>Argumentative Passages:</strong> Persuasive essays presenting opposing viewpoints on public policy, technology, or ethics. Questions test your ability to separate the author's stance from quoted counter-arguments.</li>
<li><strong>Narrative Passages:</strong> Account-based passages detailing personal experiences, historical events, or fictional accounts. They focus on sequence, cause-and-effect, and character motivation.</li>
<li><strong>Descriptive Passages:</strong> Imagery-heavy descriptions depicting scenes, places, or cultural practices. Questions target figurative language and sensory vocabulary.</li>
<li><strong>Cloze Test Passages:</strong> A continuous passage spanning roughly 200 words with 10 blank spaces. You must select the precise word that maintains grammatical continuity and thematic logic.</li>
<li><strong>Prescribed UTME Novel:</strong> A specific prose text mandated by JAMB for the examination cycle (e.g., <em>The Lekki Headmaster</em> by Kabir Alabi Garba or <em>The Life Changer</em> by Khadija Abubakar Jalli). Ten direct questions evaluate character roles, plot twists, setting, and thematic significance.</li>
</ul>

<div class="callout tip">
<strong class="label">Pro Tip</strong>
When tackling Cloze Tests, read the entire paragraph once before filling in any blank. Understanding the overall context prevents choosing words that fit locally inside a single sentence but contradict the broader paragraph meaning.
</div>

<h4>2.1.2. Key Skills Tested</h4>
<p>JAMB comprehension questions go far beyond direct factual extraction. You will encounter four primary skill assessments:</p>

<ol class="steps">
<li><strong>Direct Recall:</strong> Locating explicit statements made directly within the body text.</li>
<li><strong>Inference &amp; Logical Deduction:</strong> Arriving at valid conclusions that are implied by the text but not explicitly written out.</li>
<li><strong>Author's Tone and Attitude:</strong> Determining whether the author is sarcastic, objective, critical, pessimistic, or optimistic toward the subject matter.</li>
<li><strong>Vocabulary in Context:</strong> Defining specific words based on how they function in the passage rather than relying on dictionary definitions.</li>
</ol>

<p>Vocabulary questions measure the depth and precision of your lexicon. JAMB evaluates your mastery through synonyms, antonyms, idioms, proverbs, and fixed collocations.</p>

Always read the instruction line carefully; misreading "opposite in meaning" as "nearest in meaning" is one of the most common causes of mark loss.</p>

<h4>2.2.2. Idioms and Figurative Language</h4>
<p>Idioms are expressions whose meanings cannot be deduced from the literal meanings of their individual constituent words. JAMB tests idioms within context sentences to verify if candidates recognize figurative usage.</p>

<blockquote class="def">
<b>Idiomatic Expression:</b> A phrase where the cumulative meaning is figurative and fixed, independent of the literal definitions of the individual words (e.g., "to bury the hatchet" means to end a quarrel, not to dig a hole for a tool).
</blockquote>

<ul>
<li><strong>To burn the candle at both ends:</strong> To exhaust one's energy by working late at night and rising early.</li>
<li><strong>To steal a march on someone:</strong> To gain an unexpected advantage over a competitor quietly or secretly.</li>
<li><strong>To lock horns with:</strong> To engage in a fierce argument or conflict.</li>
<li><strong>A fly in the ointment:</strong> A minor annoyance or drawback that spoils an otherwise pleasant situation.</li>
<li><strong>To pay through the nose:</strong> To pay an exorbitant or excessive price for something.</li>
</ul>

<h4>2.2.3. Collocations and Fixed Prepositional Pairs</h4>
<p>Collocations represent natural word combinations in standard English. Misusing prepositions following specific verbs, adjectives, or nouns is a frequent trap in Lexis and Structure.</p>

<div class="callout warn">
<strong class="label">Important Rule</strong>
In standard English, you do not say "congratulate for." The correct collocation is <strong>congratulate on</strong>. Similarly, one is <strong>accused of</strong> a crime (not "accused for") and <strong>charged with</strong> an offense.
</div>

<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Prefer... to:</strong> "I prefer rice <em>to</em> beans" (NOT "I prefer rice <em>than</em> beans").</li>
<li><strong>Insist... on:</strong> "He insisted <em>on</em> paying the bill" (NOT "He insisted <em>to</em> pay").</li>
<li><strong>Abide... by:</strong> "Citizens must abide <em>by</em> the law" (NOT "abide <em>with</em>").</li>
<li><strong>Conform... to/with:</strong> "The project must conform <em>to</em> regulatory safety standards."</li>
<li><strong>Adjacent... to:</strong> "The library is situated adjacent <em>to</em> the administrative hall."</li>
</ul>

<h3>2.3. Grammar and Usage</h3>
<p>Grammar forms the structural core of Section B. Achieving perfection in this sub-section requires mastering standard grammatical rules, verb mechanics, sentence structure, and punctuation.</p>

<h4>2.3.1. Master Concord Rules (Subject-Verb Agreement)</h4>
<p>Concord dictates that a verb must agree with its subject in number and person. JAMB frequently tests advanced concord rules where basic intuition fails.</p>

<div class="status-rail">
<div class="status-step">
<span class="tag">Rule 01</span>
<h4>Proximity Rule</h4>
<p>When subjects are joined by <em>either... or</em>, <em>neither... nor</em>, or <em>not only... but also</em>, the verb agrees with the subject closest to it. Example: "Neither the principal nor the <strong>teachers were</strong> present."</p>
</div>
<div class="status-step">
<span class="tag">Rule 02</span>
<h4>Accompaniment Principles</h4>
<p>Phrases like <em>as well as</em>, <em>together with</em>, <em>along with</em>, or <em>in addition to</em> do not pluralize a singular subject. Example: "The Chairman, <strong>as well as</strong> his directors, <strong>is</strong> attending."</p>
</div>
<div class="status-step">
<span class="tag">Rule 03</span>
<h4>Indefinite Pronoun Agreement</h4>
<p>Words like <em>everyone</em>, <em>everybody</em>, <em>somebody</em>, <em>nobody</em>, <em>each</em>, and <em>either of</em> take singular verbs. Example: "<strong>Each</strong> of the candidates <strong>has</strong> submitted a form."</p>
</div>
<div class="status-step negative">
<span class="tag">Common Error</span>
<h4>Plural Attraction Error</h4>
<p>Do not let an intervening plural noun trick you into using a plural verb. Incorrect: "The list of successful candidates <em>have</em> been published." Correct: "The list of successful candidates <strong>has</strong> been published."</p>
</div>
</div>

<h4>2.3.2. Detailed Breakdown of Concord Rules</h4>
<ol class="steps">
<li><strong>Mandative Subjunctive Concord:</strong> Verbs expressing demand, recommendation, or insistence require the base form of the verb without "-s" or modal auxiliaries. Example: "The committee recommended that he <strong>resign</strong> immediately" (NOT "resigns" or "should resign").</li>
<li><strong>"Many a" Concord:</strong> The expression "Many a" is followed by a singular noun and a singular verb. Example: "<strong>Many a candidate has</strong> fallen victim to poor time management."</li>
<li><strong>More Than One Rule:</strong> "More than one" takes a singular noun and a singular verb. Example: "<strong>More than one student was</strong> penalized." However, "More students than one" takes a plural verb: "<strong>More students than one were</strong> penalized."</li>
<li><strong>Plural Nouns of Measurement:</strong> Sums of money, distances, time periods, and quantities representing a single unified concept take singular verbs. Example: "<strong>Fifty thousand naira is</strong> too expensive for the textbook."</li>
<li><strong>Fractions and Percentages:</strong> The verb agreement depends on the noun in the prepositional phrase following the fraction. Example: "Two-thirds of the <strong>building was</strong> painted," but "Two-thirds of the <strong>students were</strong> present."</li>
</ol>

<h4>2.3.3. Sentence Structures and Clause Analysis</h4>
<p>Questions in this category evaluate your understanding of dependent (subordinate) and independent clauses, relative pronouns, and sentence types.</p>

<blockquote class="def">
<b>Relative Clause:</b> A subordinate clause that modifies a noun or pronoun, typically introduced by relative pronouns such as <i>who</i>, <i>whom</i>, <i>whose</i>, <i>which</i>, or <i>that</i>.
</blockquote>

<ul>
<li><strong>Who vs. Whom:</strong> Use <strong>who</strong> when replacing a subject pronoun (he, she, they). Use <strong>whom</strong> when replacing an object pronoun (him, her, them). Example: "The student <em>who</em> scored highest was awarded a scholarship." / "The applicant <em>whom</em> the panel interviewed was qualified."</li>
<li><strong>Nominal Relative Clauses:</strong> Clauses acting as noun equivalents. Example: "<em>What he said</em> surprised everyone."</li>
<li><strong>Adverbial Clauses of Concession:</strong> Introduced by <em>although</em>, <em>even though</em>, or <em>whereas</em>. Never pair <em>although</em> with <em>but</em> in the same sentence. Incorrect: "Although he studied hard, but he failed." Correct: "<strong>Although</strong> he studied hard, he failed."</li>
</ul>

<h4>2.4.4. Word Stress and Emphatic Stress</h4>
<p>Word stress involves pronouncing a specific syllable in a word with greater force and higher pitch. Emphatic stress identifies contrastive emphasis within a sentence.</p>

<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Two-Syllable Nouns vs. Verbs:</strong> Most two-syllable nouns take primary stress on the first syllable (**PRES-ent**, **EX-port**, **PRO-duce**). Most two-syllable verbs take stress on the second syllable (pres-**ENT**, ex-**PORT**, pro-**DUCE**).</li>
<li><strong>Words Ending in "-tion", "-sion", or "-ic":</strong> Stress falls on the penultimate (second to last) syllable. Examples: edu-**CA**-tion, deci-**SION**, dynam-**IC**.</li>
<li><strong>Emphatic Stress Rule:</strong> When a word in a sentence is capitalized for emphatic stress, the correct option must directly contradict or correct the capitalized word.</li>
</ul>

<div class="callout tip">
<strong class="label">Pro Tip</strong>
For Emphatic Stress questions, locate the option that introduces a new fact specifically replacing the capitalized word while leaving the rest of the sentence structure intact.
</div>

<h3>2.5. Literary Appreciation</h3>
<p>While Literature-in-English is a separate subject, Use of English candidates are expected to identify basic figures of speech and poetic devices within comprehension passages.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Metaphor:</strong> Direct comparison without using "as" or "like" (e.g., "His words were a dagger to her heart").</li>
<li><strong>Simile:</strong> Comparison using "like" or "as" (e.g., "He fought like a lion").</li>
<li><strong>Personification:</strong> Giving human attributes to inanimate objects (e.g., "The wind whispered through the trees").</li>
<li><strong>Oxymoron:</strong> Placing two contradictory terms side-by-side (e.g., "deafening silence," "open secret").</li>
<li><strong>Hyperbole:</strong> Intentional exaggeration for emphasis (e.g., "I have told you a million times").</li>
<li><strong>Irony:</strong> A situation or expression where the intended meaning is the exact opposite of its literal meaning.</li>
<li><strong>Synecdoche:</strong> Using a part of something to represent the whole (e.g., "All hands on deck").</li>
<li><strong>Metonymy:</strong> Substituting the name of an attribute or adjunct for that of the thing meant (e.g., "The Crown announced a new law").</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="study-strategies-jamb-english">
<h2>4. Effective Study Strategies for JAMB English</h2>

<p>Studying for UTME English requires structured practice rather than passive reading. Implement the following strategies to optimize your revision.</p>

<h3>4.1. Creating a Structured Weekly Reading Schedule</h3>
<p>Divide your preparation into manageable weekly targets covering every major section of the syllabus:</p>

<ol class="steps">
<li><strong>Week 1:</strong> Prescribed UTME Novel summary, character analysis, and chapter-by-chapter review.</li>
<li><strong>Week 2:</strong> Grammar mechanics—Concord rules, Question Tags, and Subjunctive mood.</li>
<li><strong>Week 3:</strong> Vocabulary expansion—Synonyms, Antonyms, Fixed Collocations, and Idiomatic expressions.</li>
<li><strong>Week 4:</strong> Oral English drills—Vowels, Consonants, Rhymes, and Silent letters.</li>
<li><strong>Week 5:</strong> Stress patterns—Word Stress, Penultimate rule, and Emphatic Stress.</li>
<li><strong>Week 6:</strong> Full-length timed practice tests using past questions.</li>
</ol>

<h3>4.2. Active Reading Techniques for Comprehension</h3>
<p>When tackling comprehension passages during CBT practice:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read the questions first:</strong> Skim the question stems before reading the text. This helps you actively search for specific answers while reading.</li>
<li><strong>Identify Topic Sentences:</strong> The first sentence of each paragraph usually contains the main idea.</li>
<li><strong>Track Transition Words:</strong> Words like <em>however</em>, <em>nevertheless</em>, <em>consequently</em>, and <em>furthermore</em> signal shifts in argument or logical conclusions.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4.3. Building an Exam-Focused Vocabulary Notebook</h3>
<p>Maintain a dedicated lexicon journal. Whenever you encounter an unfamiliar word in past questions or practice tests, record its dictionary definition, contextual synonym, antonym, and an original example sentence.</p>

<h3>4.4. Common Pitfalls to Avoid in UTME English</h3>
<div class="callout warn">
<strong class="label">Warning: Frequent Student Errors</strong>
<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Translating from local dialects:</strong> Expressing sentences based on vernacular translation leads to errors in preposition usage (e.g., saying "I am hearing the smell" instead of "I can smell").</li>
<li><strong>Misinterpreting Antonym Instructions:</strong> Accidentally selecting a synonym when the question specifically asks for an opposite meaning.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring Silent Letters in Phonetics:</strong> Sounding out silent consonants (like pronouncing the 'b' in <em>subtle</em>) leads to incorrect choices in Oral English.</li>
<li><strong>Spending over 15 minutes on Comprehension:</strong> Spending too much time on a difficult passage leaves insufficient time for Lexis and Oral English.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<section id="faq">
<h2>7. Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<details class="faq">
<summary>How many questions are set in JAMB Use of English?</summary>
<p>JAMB Use of English consists of exactly 60 multiple-choice questions divided across three core sections: Comprehension/Summary (25 questions), Lexis and Structure (25 questions), and Oral Forms (10 questions).</p>
</details>

<details class="faq">
<summary>Is the JAMB novel compulsory for all candidates?</summary>
<p>Yes. The prescribed UTME novel is mandatory for all candidates regardless of their chosen course or subject combination. Ten questions in Section A test your understanding of the text.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq">
<summary>How can I score above 80 in JAMB English?</summary>
<p>Focus on mastering the 15 essential concord rules, study the prescribed novel thoroughly, learn basic phonetic symbols for vowel and consonant sounds, and practice timed CBT past questions regularly.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq">
<summary>Are figures of speech tested in JAMB English?</summary>
<p>Yes. Although Literature-in-English is a separate subject, basic figures of speech like metaphors, similes, irony, oxymorons, and personification are frequently tested in the comprehension and sentence interpretation sections of Use of English.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq">
<summary>What is the difference between word stress and emphatic stress?</summary>
<p>Word stress identifies which syllable within an individual word is pronounced with greater force. Emphatic stress identifies a capitalized word in a full sentence to highlight contrastive emphasis.</p>
</details>
